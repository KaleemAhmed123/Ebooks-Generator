## Judgement, intuition, expertise

[S1] Conditions for intuitive expertise: A failure to disagree — Kahneman & Klein, American Psychologist 64(6):515–526 — 2009-09 — https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016755 — study (theoretical review) — Grade A
     Intuitive judgement is trustworthy only when the environment is predictable enough and the person has had adequate opportunity to learn its regularities.

[S2] Educating Intuition — Robin Hogarth, University of Chicago Press — 2001 — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_M._Hogarth (summary of the book's thesis) — book — Grade B
     "Kind" learning environments (repeated judgements, fast clear feedback) build good intuition; "wicked" ones (missing, slow, or biased feedback) build bad intuition.

[S3] Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: a meta-analysis — Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, Psychological Science — 2014-07 — https://www.princeton.edu/news/2014/07/03/becoming-expert-takes-more-practice — meta-analysis (88 studies) — Grade A
     Deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% music, 18% sports, 4% education, and under 1% in professions. ★

[S4] Outcome bias in decision evaluation — Baron & Hershey, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 54:569–579 — 1988 — https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/baron-hershey-jpsp1988.pdf — study (5 experiments) — Grade A
     People rate the same decision as better thought-out, and its maker as more competent, when the outcome happened to be good.

[S5] Outcomes affect evaluations of decision quality: replication and extensions of Baron & Hershey (1988) Experiment 1 — PMC12372742 — 2025 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12372742/ — pre-registered replication — Grade A
     Outcome bias replicates with a larger sample.

## Forecasting and calibration

[S6] Expert Political Judgment: How Good Is It? How Can We Know? — Philip Tetlock, Princeton University Press — 2005 — https://scholars-stage.org/foxes-hedgehogs-and-forecasting/ (secondary summary) — book (20-year study, 284 experts) — Grade A (book) / B (summary used)
     Experts' long-range political forecasts were only slightly better than chance and worse than simple statistical models; "foxes" (eclectic, self-correcting) beat "hedgehogs" (one big theory).

[S7] Psychological strategies for winning a geopolitical forecasting tournament — Mellers et al., Psychological Science 25(5):1106–1115 — 2014-03 — https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797614524255/ — study (2-year tournament, IARPA) — Grade A
     Probability training, teaming, and tracking each improved calibration and resolution. Training covered reference classes, bias correction, and averaging estimates.

[S8] Developing expert political judgment: the impact of training and practice on judgmental accuracy in geopolitical forecasting tournaments — Chang, Chen, Mellers & Tetlock, Judgment and Decision Making 11(5):509–526 — 2016-09 — https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/judgdm/v11y2016i5p509-526_8.html — study (4 years) — Grade A
     A sub-one-hour debiasing module ("CHAMPS KNOW") improved Brier scores 6–11% over control; cognitive ability and practice contributed independently.

[S9] Identifying and cultivating superforecasters as a method of improving probabilistic predictions — Mellers et al., Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(3):267–281 — 2015 — https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691615577794/ — study — Grade A
     Top-2% forecasters placed in elite teams did not regress to the mean over two years; explained by ability, task skill, motivation, and enriched environments.

[S10] Timid choices and bold forecasts: a cognitive perspective on risk taking — Kahneman & Lovallo, Management Science — 1993 — https://www.lesswrong.com/w/inside-outside-view (secondary summary of the concept) — study/theory — Grade A (paper) / B (summary used)
     Introduces "inside view" (reasoning from case details) vs "outside view" (the record of similar past cases).

[S11] From Nobel Prize to project management: getting risks right — Bent Flyvbjerg, Project Management Journal 37(3) — 2006-08 — https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/nobel-project-management-reference-class-forecasting-8068 — essay with data — Grade B
     Reference-class forecasting: base the estimate on actual outcomes of comparable past projects, bypassing optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation.

## Software estimation

[S12] From origami to software development: a review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time — Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen, Psychological Bulletin 138(2):238–271 — 2012 — https://www.simula.no/publications/origami-software-development-review-studies-judgment-based-predictions-performance-time — systematic review — Grade A
     The authoritative review of time/effort prediction across domains, software included. (Cited for scope; specific numbers taken from S13–S14.)

[S13] Jørgensen et al., studies of minimum–maximum effort intervals (Simula Research Laboratory) — 2002–2004 — https://web-backend.simula.no/sites/default/files/publications/Simula.simula.2500.pdf — studies — Grade A
     Across 18 projects, only 64% of "90% confidence" effort intervals contained the actual effort. Student programmers' 98% intervals hit ~60% (Connolly & Dean, cited therein). ★

[S14] The role of outcome feedback in improving the uncertainty assessment of software development effort estimates — Gruschke & Jørgensen, Simula — ~2008 — https://www.simula.no/research/role-outcome-feedback-improving-uncertainty-assessment-software-development-effort — study (5 students + 10 professionals) — Grade A (small n)
     Most developers were overconfident and stayed overconfident despite repeated, timely outcome feedback. A related Simula study: 30-minute lessons-learned sessions did not improve estimation accuracy or interval realism. ★

## Decision tools

[S15] Back to the future: temporal perspective in the explanation of events — Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, Journal of Behavioral Decision Making 2(1) — 1989 — https://colab.ws/articles/10.1002%2Fbdm.3960020103 — study — Grade A
     Origin of "prospective hindsight". The abstract reports outcome *certainty*, more than temporal perspective, shapes explanations: sure events get longer, more episodic explanations.

[S16] Evaluating the effectiveness of the PreMortem technique on plan confidence — Veinott, Klein & Wiggins, ISCRAM proceedings — 2010 — https://corporate.jasoncollins.blog/premortem (secondary summary) — study (178 students, hypothetical plan) — Grade B (conference paper, single study, summary used)
     Premortem cut overconfidence in a plan more than critique, pros/cons, or cons-only conditions.

[S17] Debiasing decisions: improved decision making with a single training intervention — Morewedge et al., Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences — 2015 — https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/12324 — study (2 longitudinal experiments) — Grade A
     One training session cut six biases; games (personalised feedback + practice) beat videos; effects persisted ≥2 months and generalised to untaught formats. ★

[S18] Debiasing training improves decision making in the field — Sellier, Scopelliti & Morewedge, Psychological Science — 2019-09 — https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/debiasing-your-decisions.html — field experiment (N=290 graduate students) — Grade A
     One-shot game-based training reduced confirmation-biased choices in an unannounced business case by almost a third.

[S19] Before you make that big decision… — Kahneman, Lovallo & Sibony, Harvard Business Review — 2011-06 — https://faculty.washington.edu/pathaksd/BBUS549/Required%20Readings/Before%20You%20Make%20that%20Big%20Decision.pdf — practitioner article — Grade B
     A 12-question checklist for reviewers of a proposal; cites a McKinsey study of 1,000+ investments where bias-reducing process was associated with returns up to 7 percentage points higher (correlational, consultancy data).

[S20] ShadowBox training evaluations — Klein et al. (gary-klein.com; Cognition, Technology & Work 2018) — 2015–2018 — https://www.gary-klein.com/sb — studies — Grade B (developer-run evaluations)
     Scenario-based training where trainees compare their choices and rationale to an expert panel's: +28% match to expert rankings (Marines, N=59, 3h); 26% better root-cause identification on a plant simulator.

[S21] Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure — Google SRE Book, ch. 15 — 2016 — https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/ — original docs — Grade A (as a description of practice) / B (as evidence of effect)
     Defines postmortem triggers in advance; blameless writing so contributing causes surface; goal is preventive action.

[S22] Expectations, outcomes, and challenges of modern code review — Bacchelli & Bird, ICSE — 2013 — https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=164195 — study (Microsoft: 873 programmers + 165 managers surveyed, 17 interviews, 570 comments) — Grade A
     Defect-finding is the stated motive, but reviews mostly yield code improvements, knowledge transfer, and awareness; understanding the change is the main difficulty.

## Automation bias and AI output

[S23] Complacency and bias in human use of automation: an attentional integration — Parasuraman & Manzey, Human Factors 52(3) — 2010 — https://depositonce.tu-berlin.de/items/0c787d91-6021-480b-a333-009e0d1d0092/full — review — Grade A
     Automation bias produces omission and commission errors with imperfect aids; it hits experts and novices alike and is not prevented by training or instructions alone.

[S24] Accountability and automation bias — Skitka, Mosier & Burdick, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies — 2000 — https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20020041010 — study (students and professional pilots) — Grade A
     People who felt accountable for *how* they used the aid verified it more and made fewer automation errors.

[S25] Automation bias: a systematic review of frequency, effect mediators, and mitigators — Goddard, Roudsari & Wyatt, JAMIA 19(1):121–127 — 2012 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751 — systematic review — Grade A
     Defines automation bias as the tendency to over-rely on automation; reviews mediators and mitigations, mostly in healthcare.

[S26] Asleep at the keyboard? Assessing the security of GitHub Copilot's code contributions — Pearce, Ahmad, Tan, Dolan-Gavitt & Karri, IEEE S&P 2022; CACM 68(2) 2025 — https://cacm.acm.org/author/hammond-pearce — study — Grade A
     89 security-relevant scenarios drawn from the 2021 CWE Top 25; Copilot completions were frequently vulnerable. (Headline rate not quoted here: search extracts gave conflicting figures.) ★

[S27] Do users write more insecure code with AI assistants? — Perry, Srivastava, Kumar & Boneh, ACM CCS — 2023-11 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622 — user study (N=47) — Grade A
     AI-assisted participants wrote significantly less secure code *and* were more likely to believe it was secure; those who trusted the AI less and reworked prompts produced fewer vulnerabilities.

[S28] Lost at C: a user study on the security implications of large language model code assistants — Sandoval et al., USENIX Security — 2023 — https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity23/presentation/sandoval — user study (N=58, C) — Grade A
     Counter-case: in a low-level C task, AI-assisted users produced critical security bugs at a rate no more than 10% above control.

[S29] Is GitHub's Copilot as bad as humans at introducing vulnerabilities in code? — Asare, Nagappan & Asokan, Empirical Software Engineering — 2023 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04741 — study — Grade A
     Counter-case: replaying real human-introduced C/C++ vulnerabilities, Copilot reproduced the vulnerable code ~33% of the time and the fix ~25%; "not as bad as humans".

[S30] The impact of generative AI on critical thinking — Lee, Sarkar et al. (Microsoft Research), CHI — 2025 — https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1135061 — survey (319 knowledge workers, 936 examples) — Grade B (self-report)
     Higher confidence in GenAI associated with less critical thinking; higher self-confidence with more. Critical work shifts to verification, integration, and stewardship.

[S31] Navigating the jagged technological frontier — Dell'Acqua et al., HBS working paper — 2023 — https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf — pre-registered field experiment (758 BCG consultants) — Grade A
     On a task chosen to sit outside GPT-4's capability, consultants with AI were 19 percentage points less likely to be correct than those without.

[S32] Measuring the impact of early-2025 AI on experienced open-source developer productivity — METR — 2025-07 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089 — RCT (16 devs, 246 tasks) — Grade A (small n)
     Developers forecast AI would cut task time 24%; with AI they took 19% longer — and afterward still believed it had sped them up.

[S33] METR late-2025 follow-up and design change — METR (via secondary reporting) — 2026 — https://conffab.com/?p=10163 — study update — Grade B
     Follow-up point estimates showed speedups (returning devs ~18%, wide CI) but METR called the signal unreliable: developers increasingly refused to work without AI, biasing the sample.

[S34] How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills — Anthropic — 2026-01 — https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills — RCT (52 mostly junior engineers learning a new Python library) — Grade A (lab RCT; vendor-run, small n)
     AI group scored 50% vs 67% on a follow-up quiz, largest gap on debugging; those using AI for conceptual questions scored ≥65%, those delegating code generation <40%. Speed gain not significant.

[S35] Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: evidence from high school mathematics — Bastani et al., PNAS 122(26) — 2025 — https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html — field experiment (~1,000 students) — Grade A
     Unrestricted GPT-4 raised practice scores 48% but cut later unassisted exam scores 17%; a tutor version with guardrails largely removed the harm.
