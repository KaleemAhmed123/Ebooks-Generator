## mTLS — both sides prove identity

- Ordinary TLS is one-sided: the **client** verifies the **server** (you check the bank's certificate; the bank doesn't check yours at the TLS layer — it uses a password later). **Mutual TLS (mTLS)** makes it symmetric: the client **also** presents a certificate, and the server verifies it. Now each end cryptographically proves who it is before any data flows.
- Why infrastructure cares: inside a cluster, "who is calling this service?" shouldn't rest on network position (being on the same subnet proves nothing — Booklet 11's zero-trust argument). mTLS gives every service a verifiable **identity**, so service B can refuse anyone who isn't service A, regardless of where the packet came from.

- This is the engine of a **service mesh** (Istio, Linkerd, Cilium — Booklets 6 and 11): a sidecar or node proxy gives every workload an identity certificate, rotates it automatically every few hours, and wraps all service-to-service traffic in mTLS **without the application knowing**. Encryption and authentication become a platform property, not something each team re-implements.
- The cost is **certificate lifecycle** at scale — issuing, distributing, and rotating thousands of short-lived certs. That's why identity frameworks (SPIFFE/SPIRE) and automation (cert-manager) exist; doing it by hand does not survive contact with a real cluster.
