# About the author

**Kaleem Ahmed** builds backend systems in Node.js, TypeScript and Python — the kind where a wrong assumption about ordering or concurrency shows up as a silent data loss three weeks later. Much of what he knows about algorithms came from those three weeks, not from a textbook.

He has solved more than 1,500 competitive programming problems, reached a peak LeetCode rating of 1,798, and placed 812th globally in Weekly Contest 359. None of that is the point. The point is that somewhere around problem 400 he stopped memorising solutions and started deriving them, and every problem after that got easier. This book is the method he arrived at, written down before he could forget why it worked.

His approach to explanation is simple: state the idea, show the smallest code that proves it, name the failure mode that will cost you thirty minutes of debugging if nobody warns you. Then stop. He treats every extra sentence as a cost, not a courtesy. If a diagram can replace a paragraph, the paragraph goes.

He writes because writing is where he discovers whether he understood something or merely recognised it. A clean explanation usually means the idea is settled. One that keeps needing qualifiers usually means it is not. Several pages in this book went through that cycle more times than he would like to admit.

He uses AI as a multiplier, never as a substitute. Every line still gets reviewed. Every merge still has a name on it. The engineer who ships the change is the one who answers for it.

Away from code he travels, plays snooker, and picks apart puzzles for no reason beyond the fact that they are interesting. Unfamiliar cities and unfamiliar problems reward the same instinct: stay curious, stay patient, and say so when you do not understand something yet.
